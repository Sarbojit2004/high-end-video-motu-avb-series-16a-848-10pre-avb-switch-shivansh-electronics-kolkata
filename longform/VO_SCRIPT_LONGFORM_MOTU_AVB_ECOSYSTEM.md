# Voiceover script — MOTU AVB Series Ecosystem (long-form, 898 s / 14:58)

**Language:** English only. Narration is recorded separately and dropped into
`public/vo/voiceover-longform.mp3`. There are **no burned-in captions** in the video.

## Tone

A **blend, weighted toward Brief Stage 9's "Precise & Technical-but-Accessible"
(The Systems Engineer)**, opened and closed in **"Warm & Trustworthy" (The
Infrastructure Specialist)**.

Why this blend rather than one of the three as written: the buyer here is a
facility director or chief engineer spending ₹1,87,900 a unit on infrastructure,
so the body of the video has to survive technical scrutiny — exact figures,
correct terminology, no hype. But the *opening* problem and the *closing*
invitation are not technical arguments, they are recognition and reassurance,
and the Systems Engineer voice is too cool for either. "Cinematic & Aspirational"
was left out deliberately: at fifteen minutes it would wear thin, and it invites
the kind of superlative claim this project cannot make.

**Delivery:** unhurried, ~150 words per minute, consultative. Land the numbers
cleanly and let them sit. Never sell — explain, and let the pricing do its own work.

**Pacing note:** each block below is budgeted at roughly 2.5 words/second against
its beat length, leaving deliberate air. Timecodes are the beat boundaries in the
rendered video; narration should start ~0.4 s after each boundary so the visual
transition reads first.

## Chapter 1 — The Hook & The Problem (00:00–01:30)

**[00:00–00:12] Cold open**
> Every studio starts with one room. One interface. One set of decisions. And for
> a while, that is genuinely all you need.

**[00:12–00:22] One room**
> In a single room, the signal path is easy to hold in your head. One converter.
> One clock. Everything you record sounds like everything else you record.

**[00:22–00:32] The second room**
> Then you add a second room. And almost immediately, the questions start
> compounding faster than the channel count does.

**[00:32–00:46] Converter roulette**
> The usual answer is a cheaper second interface for the new room. It works —
> until you discover the vocal booth has different headroom and a different
> distortion signature to the live room. You find out at mixdown, which is the
> worst possible time to find out.

**[00:46–00:59] The scaling wall**
> The alternatives are not much kinder. Optical chaining brings clocking errors
> that multiply with every device. Aggregate device workarounds destabilise the
> host. And proprietary audio-over-IP wants licensing fees and its own switches.

**[00:59–01:11] The compromise purchase**
> So most engineers compromise. Enough microphone preamps to track a kit almost
> always meant giving up the balanced line inputs you need for outboard and
> synthesisers. You buy the box that half-fits, and you work around it.

**[01:11–01:20] Branding beat**
> *(hold — visual only)*

**[01:20–01:30] The turn**
> None of that is actually inevitable. It is a consequence of buying boxes, one
> at a time, instead of building a system.

## Chapter 2 — The Thesis: One Engine, Three Front-Ends (01:30–04:00)

**[01:30–01:38] Chapter open**
> The MOTU AVB Series starts from the opposite end of the problem.

**[01:38–01:54] The triptych**
> Three interfaces. The 16A, the 848, and the 10pre. They are not a good, better,
> best ladder. They are three answers to the question of what a particular room
> physically needs — and they carry the same Market Operating Price, one lakh
> eighty-seven thousand nine hundred rupees per unit, inclusive of GST.

**[01:54–02:08] The shared engine**
> That price is identical because the engine is identical. Not similar —
> identical. The same thirty-two-bit floating point DSP, the same conversion, the
> same mixer, in all three chassis.

**[02:08–02:18] Badges**
> ESS Sabre32 Ultra conversion. Thunderbolt 4 and USB4 connectivity. A sixty-four
> channel CueMix Pro mixer. In every one of them.

**[02:18–02:31] Shared specification**
> Sample rates from forty-four point one all the way to one hundred and
> ninety-two kilohertz. Whichever front-end the signal enters through, the
> mathematics applied to it are the same mathematics.

**[02:31–02:43] Round-trip latency**
> Round-trip latency is approximately one point eight milliseconds, measured at
> ninety-six kilohertz on a thirty-two sample host buffer. That figure does not
> change between the three units, because there is nothing between them to change.

**[02:43–02:54] CueMix Pro**
> CueMix Pro is a large-format console running on the interface itself. Sixty-four
> inputs, thirty-two buses, with four-band double-precision EQ, compression,
> gating and a high-pass filter on every single channel.

**[02:54–03:06] Zero-latency monitoring**
> Because that processing lives on the hardware and not on your computer, the host
> buffer size stops dictating what the performer hears. You can run a large buffer
> for a heavy mix and still monitor with no perceptible delay.

**[03:06–03:17] Thunderbolt**
> Connection to the computer is Thunderbolt 4 or USB4 over a single Type-C cable —
> forty gigabits per second, and up to two hundred and fifty-six channels of host
> input and output.

**[03:17–03:30] Identical pricing**
> Which brings us back to the price. When the engine is identical and the price is
> identical, the decision stops being about quality. It becomes a much simpler
> question: which physical connections does this room actually need?

**[03:30–03:39] Branding beat**
> *(hold — visual only)*

**[03:39–03:50] Network preview**
> And there is one more thing every unit shares. A two-port AVB switch, built into
> the chassis. The first room you add costs you nothing in extra networking

**[03:50–04:00] Transformation**

## Chapter 3 — Capture: The MOTU 10pre (04:00–06:30)

**[04:00–04:08] Chapter open**
> So let us take the three front-ends one at a time, starting where the signal does.

**[04:08–04:24] Hero reveal**
> The 10pre is the tracking specialist. Where the other two allocate their front
> panel to routing and to monitoring, the 10pre spends almost all of its internal
> real estate on one thing: getting microphones into the digital domain cleanly,
> and a lot of them at once.

**[04:24–04:38] Inserts**
> Channels one and two carry dedicated send and return inserts, so an outboard
> preamp, compressor or EQ can sit in the path before conversion — on exactly the
> two channels most likely to want one.

**[04:38–04:51] The preamps**
> Ten microphone preamplifiers. Up to seventy-four decibels of gain, in one
> decibel increments. Equivalent input noise of minus one hundred and twenty-nine
> dBu, and distortion at minus one hundred and fourteen dB THD plus N.

**[04:51–05:05] Rear sweep**

**[05:05–05:17] Front / rear split**

**[05:17–05:27] Metering**

**[05:27–05:39] The full unit**

**[05:39–05:51] Software**

**[05:51–06:02] Routing**

**[06:02–06:12] Details**

## Chapter 4 — Routing: The MOTU 16A (06:30–09:00)

**[06:30–06:38] Chapter open**
> If the 10pre is where audio enters the building, the 16A is where it gets
> organised.

**[06:38–06:52] Hero reveal**
> The 16A is the routing specialist, and it makes an unusual choice: it has no
> microphone preamplifiers at all. That is deliberate. It assumes your preamps are
> already in the rack, and it gives the space to line-level connectivity instead.

**[06:52–07:07] Port density sweep**
> Sixteen quarter-inch TRS line inputs. Sixteen quarter-inch TRS line outputs. All
> balanced, all on the rear panel, in one continuous bank — the density is the
> entire point of the unit.

**[07:07–07:19] Specification**
> Thirty-two inputs and thirty-four outputs. Sixty-six simultaneous channels, more
> than either of the other two front-ends, in the same single rack unit.

**[07:19–07:32] Dual display**
> And uniquely in this ecosystem, the 16A carries two three point nine inch RGB
> displays, so every analogue and digital bank can be metered at the same time
> rather than switched between.

**[07:32–07:45] DC-coupled outputs**
> Here is the specification that surprises people. The line outputs are
> DC-coupled — there are no blocking capacitors in the path. Which means the 16A
> can route control voltage from your DAW straight into a modular synthesiser,
> exactly as readily as it routes audio.

**[07:45–07:55] Optical expansion**
> Optical adds sixteen channels of ADAT at forty-four point one and forty-eight
> kilohertz, or eight channels of S/MUX at the higher rates.

**[07:55–08:06] Rear panel**

**[08:06–08:17] The full unit**

**[08:17–08:28] The patchbay**

**[08:28–08:38] Software**

## Chapter 6 — The Network: AVB Switch & Milan (11:30–13:30)

**[11:30–11:38] Chapter open**
> The MOTU AVB Switch is not a fourth interface. It does not convert audio at all.

**[11:38–11:56] Topology**
> Remember that every interface has a two-port AVB switch built in. So two
> devices — a 10pre in the live room, an 848 in the control room — connect directly
> to each other, with no extra hardware and no extra cost. It is when you reach a
> third room, and a fourth, that you need something in the middle.

**[11:56–12:10] The Switch**
> That is what this is. Six gigabit AVB ports, and the point at which your building
> stops being a set of rooms and starts being one network.

**[12:10–12:22] Specification**
> It runs over standard CAT-5e or CAT-6 cable — up to one hundred metres per run.
> Ordinary Ethernet cable, from any supplier, pulled by any electrician.

**[12:22–12:33] The unit**
> Device discovery is automatic. There is no IT administration to do, and no
> proprietary switch to buy.

**[12:33–12:43] Standards**
> This is Milan-certified, on the open IEEE 802.1 AVB standard, with clock
> synchronisation handled by gPTP — accurate across the whole network to
> nanoseconds.

**[12:43–12:52] Cabling**
> Which means hundreds of channels of audio travelling between rooms on cable that
> costs a few rupees a metre, instead of analogue multicore pulled through walls.

**[12:52–13:04] Star topology**
> Rooms become nodes. Adding a room becomes a cable run and a configuration change,
> rather than a rebuild.

**[13:04–13:14] Quality of service**
> And because AVB reserves bandwidth for a stream before it carries it, your audio
> is never competing with everything else on the wire for its turn.

**[13:14–13:22] Branding beat**
> *(hold — visual only)*

**[13:22–13:30] The ceiling**
> The theoretical limits: one hundred and fifty AVB devices, across thirty-seven
> switches, carrying five hundred and twelve simultaneous streams — four thousand
> and ninety-six channels of audio.
